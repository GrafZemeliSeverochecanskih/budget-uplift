\begin{abstract}
Uplift models promise to send a promotion only to the customers it persuades, but their value is
rarely tested end to end: from a randomised experiment, through heterogeneous-effect estimation and
budget-constrained optimisation, to an unbiased estimate of the money the resulting policy earns.
We run that full pipeline on the Hillstrom e-mail experiment (64{,}000 customers, three randomised
arms) under a release protocol in which every analysis choice is frozen in a commit before it is
run. Five uplift learners fail to rank customers better than random on two-week spend, and the
model-targeted policy does not beat the one-line rule ``send the better e-mail to a random subset''
at any budget. A semi-synthetic positive control on the same covariates and assignment shows that
the same code recovers a known effect (correlation of estimated and true effects up to $0.83$;
$+\$0.50$ $\CI{0.27}{0.75}$ per customer over random targeting at a 30\% budget). A sweep over
effect heterogeneity and sample size shows why: at Hillstrom's size a single experiment detects a
real gain over the best single arm in only 5--25\% of replications unless most of the effect is
concentrated in a third of customers. We further show that (i) repeated cross-fitting narrows
policy-value intervals by 37--58\% while accounting for model-refit variance; (ii) a profit
objective selects its own budget and recovers the known optimum; (iii) doubly robust policy trees
never outperform estimate-then-optimise; (iv) plug-in chance constraints on random voucher uptake
are defeated by the optimiser's curse, which a recalibration estimated from the same experiment
cannot repair, whereas a conservative bootstrap bound holds the target at a 16--19\% profit cost;
and (v) a budgeted Thompson-sampling bandit beats or ties every experiment-then-target design. All code, configurations and results are public and
reproduce bit-for-bit from a fresh clone.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Marketing budgets are finite, so a firm that can send two different promotional e-mails must
decide \emph{who} receives \emph{which} e-mail, and how much to spend in total. Uplift modelling
\citep{radcliffe2007,gutierrez2017} answers the first question by estimating, for each customer, the
conditional average treatment effect (CATE) of each promotion and targeting those with the largest
predicted lift. A rich literature on meta-learners \citep{kunzel2019,kennedy2023,nie2021} and causal
forests \citep{wager2018,athey2019grf} makes such estimates easy to produce. Whether they are
\emph{good enough to change a decision} is a different question, and it is the one a business
cares about.

Answering it requires connecting four steps that are usually studied separately:
(1)~a randomised experiment that identifies causal effects;
(2)~a model of how those effects vary across customers;
(3)~an optimisation that turns noisy effect estimates into a feasible allocation under a budget;
(4)~an unbiased, uncertainty-quantified evaluation of the policy that results.
Errors compound along this chain. A learner that is ``better than random'' on a ranking metric may
still lose money once the budget is spent, and an optimiser fed noisy estimates systematically
over-promises \citep{smith2006}.

This paper runs the full chain on the Hillstrom e-mail experiment \citep{hillstrom2008} and reports
what survives. Our emphasis is on credibility rather than on a new estimator:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item \textbf{Pre-registration by commit.} Every release freezes its configuration (seeds, splits,
        grids, costs, budgets) in a version-control commit \emph{before} the code that uses it is run,
        in the spirit of \citet{nosek2018}. Post-result changes are logged with reasons.
  \item \textbf{Positive control.} Real covariates and real treatment assignment are combined with
        simulated outcomes whose CATE is known, so that a null result on real data can be
        distinguished from a blind spot of the method.
  \item \textbf{Honest negatives.} Learners that do not beat a baseline, intervals that do not
        cover, and constraints that do not hold are reported, not tuned away.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i) An end-to-end, multi-arm, budget-constrained uplift pipeline with doubly robust off-policy
evaluation, whose central finding on Hillstrom is negative for spend.
(ii) A semi-synthetic positive control and a heterogeneity$\times$sample-size sweep that quantify
how weak that negative evidence is.
(iii) Four extensions, each pre-registered: a profit objective with repeated cross-fitting,
doubly robust policy learning, chance-constrained budgets under random uptake, and a budgeted
bandit. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work, Section~\ref{sec:data} the data and protocol,
Section~\ref{sec:methods} the methods, Section~\ref{sec:results} the results, and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} limitations.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Uplift modelling and CATE estimation.}
Uplift modelling originated in direct marketing, where \citet{radcliffe2007} introduced the Qini
curve for evaluating lift-based targeting; \citet{gutierrez2017} connect it to the potential-outcomes
framework \citep{rubin1974,imbens2015}. Multi-treatment uplift is treated by \citet{zhao2017} and
benchmarked by \citet{olaya2020}. Meta-learners reduce CATE estimation to supervised learning: the
S-, T- and X-learners of \citet{kunzel2019}, the DR-learner \citep{kennedy2023} and the R-learner
\citep{nie2021}. Causal forests \citep{wager2018,athey2019grf} adapt random forests to effect
estimation with valid inference. Double/debiased machine learning \citep{chernozhukov2018}
provides the cross-fitting framework we use for evaluation.

\paragraph{Policy evaluation and learning.}
Inverse-propensity weighting \citep{horvitz1952} and augmented, doubly robust estimators
\citep{robins1994,dudik2011} evaluate a policy from randomised or logged data. Policy learning
maximises such estimates directly over a restricted policy class
\citep{kitagawa2018,athey2021}; we compare it against the more common estimate-then-optimise
approach.

\paragraph{Budgeted allocation.}
Assigning at most one of several costly treatments per customer under a budget is a
multiple-choice knapsack problem \citep{sinha1979,kellerer2004}; Lagrangian relaxation
\citep{everett1963} gives fast near-optimal solutions. Chance-constrained programming
\citep{charnes1959} handles random costs, and the optimiser's curse \citep{smith2006} explains why
plug-in optimisation over estimates is systematically optimistic.

\paragraph{Sequential decisions.}
Thompson sampling \citep{thompson1933,russo2018}, here in its bootstrap form \citep{eckles2014},
balances exploration and exploitation; bandits with knapsacks \citep{badanidiyuru2018} formalise
budget constraints in that setting.

\paragraph{Variance reduction in experiments.}
CUPED \citep{deng2013} and fully interacted regression adjustment \citep{lin2013} use pre-period
covariates to sharpen average-effect estimates; we use the latter as our adjusted estimator.

\section{Data and protocol}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{The Hillstrom experiment.}
The MineThatData challenge data \citep{hillstrom2008} contain $64{,}000$ customers who purchased in
the previous twelve months, randomised in equal thirds to a men's-merchandise e-mail (``Mens''),
a women's-merchandise e-mail (``Womens'') or no e-mail. Covariates are recency (months since last
purchase), past-year spend (\texttt{history}), indicators for past men's and women's purchases, a new-customer
flag, zip-code type and purchase channel. Outcomes over the following two weeks are a website visit
(base rate $14.7\%$), a conversion ($0.9\%$) and spend (mean \$1.05, heavily right-skewed; mean
\$116 among buyers). The data were obtained through \texttt{scikit-uplift} \citep{sklift} and verified
against a recorded SHA-256 hash. Balance is excellent: the largest standardised mean difference
between any treated arm and control across 11 encoded covariates is $0.014$.

\paragraph{Split.}
A single 70/30 split, stratified by arm, gives 44{,}800 training and 19{,}200 test customers.
Everything that is fitted or selected uses training data only; the test split is used once per
analysis.

\paragraph{Costs and budgets.}
Hillstrom contains no cost data. We assume \$0.10 per Mens and \$0.08 per Womens e-mail, so that the
choice of e-mail is a genuine trade-off, and express a budget as a fraction $f$ of the cost of
e-mailing every customer at the average e-mail cost, $B = f\cdot n\cdot \bar c$ with
$\bar c=\$0.09$.

\paragraph{Release protocol.}
The study was carried out as six releases (v1.0--v1.5). For each, the configuration was committed
before any code that uses it was written, and changes made after seeing results are logged with a
reason (five such changes in total, e.g.\ a replacement of a single random draw by a permutation null
as the random baseline). The complete history, configuration and a decision log are public
(\repourl).

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Setting and identification}
Customer $i$ has covariates $X_i$, receives arm $T_i\in\{0,1,2\}$ (no e-mail, Mens, Womens) and has
potential outcomes $Y_i(a)$. Under SUTVA and randomisation with known propensity $e(a)=\Prob(T=a)=1/3$,
the conditional effect of arm $a$,
\begin{equation}
  \tau_a(x) = \E\big[Y(a)-Y(0)\mid X=x\big],
\end{equation}
is identified, and the propensity is plugged in rather than estimated everywhere except in a
deliberately confounded extension.

\paragraph{Average effects.}
For arm $a$ against control we report the difference in means with a Welch interval and the
fully interacted regression adjustment of \citet{lin2013},
\begin{equation}
  Y_i = \alpha + \beta D_i + (X_i-\bar X)^{\top}\gamma + D_i (X_i-\bar X)^{\top}\delta + \varepsilon_i,
\end{equation}
where $D_i=\ind[T_i=a]$ and $\hat\beta$ estimates the ATE with HC2 standard errors; with \texttt{history}
as the only covariate this is CUPED \citep{deng2013}.

\subsection{CATE learners}
All base models are LightGBM regressors \citep{ke2017} with one parameter set per outcome, chosen by
3-fold cross-validated MSE of the outcome model on training data.
The \emph{S-learner} fits $\mu(x,a)$ on $[x,\text{onehot}(a)]$ and sets
$\tauh_a(x)=\muh(x,a)-\muh(x,0)$; the \emph{T-learner} fits one $\muh_a$ per arm. The
\emph{X-learner} \citep{kunzel2019} imputes individual effects
\begin{equation}
  \tilde D^{1}_i = Y_i-\muh_0(X_i)\ (T_i=a), \qquad \tilde D^{0}_i = \muh_a(X_i)-Y_i\ (T_i=0),
\end{equation}
regresses each on $X$ to obtain $\tauh^1_a,\tauh^0_a$, and combines them as
$\tauh_a = g\,\tauh^0_a + (1-g)\,\tauh^1_a$ with the known within-pair propensity $g=1/2$.
The \emph{DR-learner} \citep{kennedy2023} regresses the doubly robust pseudo-outcome
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma_{ia} = \muh_a(X_i) + \frac{\ind[T_i=a]}{e(a)}\big(Y_i-\muh_a(X_i)\big)
  \label{eq:gamma}
\end{equation}
difference $\Gamma_{ia}-\Gamma_{i0}$ on $X$, and the \emph{causal forest} uses CausalForestDML from
EconML \citep{econml,athey2019grf}, both with the known propensity. The learner used downstream is
chosen on a 25\% hold-out of the training data.

\paragraph{Ranking metrics.}
For arm $a$ against control, sort test customers by $\tauh_a$ and let $N_T(k),N_C(k)$ and
$Y_T(k),Y_C(k)$ be the counts and outcome sums of treated and control customers among the top $k$.
The Qini curve \citep{radcliffe2007} is
\begin{equation}
  Q(k) = Y_T(k) - Y_C(k)\,\frac{N_T(k)}{N_C(k)},
\end{equation}
and the Qini coefficient is the area between $Q$ and the line of random targeting. Its random
baseline is a permutation null over 500 random orderings; intervals are percentile bootstrap
intervals over test rows \citep{efron1993}.

\subsection{Budget-constrained allocation}
With $x_{ia}\in\{0,1\}$ indicating that customer $i$ receives arm $a\in\{1,2\}$, costs $c_a$ and
budget $B$, the allocation problem is the multiple-choice knapsack \citep{sinha1979}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \max_{x}\ \ & \textstyle\sum_{i}\sum_{a} \tauh_{ia}\, x_{ia}\\
  \text{s.t.}\ \ & \textstyle\sum_{a} x_{ia}\le 1\ \ \forall i, \qquad \sum_{i}\sum_{a} c_a\, x_{ia}\le B.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ip}
\end{equation}
We solve \eqref{eq:ip} exactly with SCIP \citep{scip8} through OR-Tools \citep{ortools}, after
removing pairs with $\tauh_{ia}\le 0$ (which cannot be in an optimal solution). The Lagrangian
heuristic \citep{everett1963} assigns
$a^\star_i(\lambda)=\argmax_{a\in\{0,1,2\}}\{\tauh_{ia}-\lambda c_a\}$ (with value $0$ for no e-mail)
and bisects $\lambda\ge 0$ until the spend fits $B$; the dual function
\begin{equation}
  L(\lambda) = \textstyle\sum_i \max\big(0,\max_a(\tauh_{ia}-\lambda c_a)\big) + \lambda B
\end{equation}
upper-bounds the optimum. From v1.1 on, leftover budget is spent greedily by $\tauh_{ia}/c_a$
(``repair''), which fixes the all-or-nothing behaviour of the heuristic on groups with tied
$\tauh$.

\subsection{Off-policy evaluation}
A (possibly stochastic) policy assigns probabilities $\pi(a\mid x)$. With known propensity, the
inverse-propensity \citep{horvitz1952} and doubly robust \citep{robins1994,dudik2011} estimates of its
value are
\begin{align}
  \hat V_{\text{IPW}}(\pi) &= \frac1n\sum_i \frac{\pi(T_i\mid X_i)}{e(T_i)}\,Y_i, \\
  \hat V_{\text{DR}}(\pi)  &= \frac1n\sum_i \Big[\sum_a \pi(a\mid X_i)\,\muh_a(X_i)
     + \frac{\pi(T_i\mid X_i)}{e(T_i)}\big(Y_i-\muh_{T_i}(X_i)\big)\Big].
  \label{eq:dr}
\end{align}
Both are unbiased for any fixed $\muh$ because $e$ is known. Random targeting and ``best single
arm'' baselines are evaluated as stochastic policies whose expected cost equals the budget, so
their values carry no Monte-Carlo noise. Incremental values are reported against sending nothing,
with paired bootstrap intervals.

\paragraph{Repeated cross-fitting (v1.2 on).}
Evaluating on a single test split ignores model-refit variance and uses only 30\% of the data. We
therefore also evaluate on all customers by $K$-fold cross-fitting \citep{chernozhukov2018}: for
each fold the learner and $\muh$ are fitted on the other folds, the policy is allocated on the
held-out fold, and the per-customer DR scores of \eqref{eq:dr} are averaged. Repeating with $R$
fold splits gives estimates $\hat\theta_r$ and standard errors $\hat\sigma_r$, combined as
\begin{equation}
  \hat\theta = \operatorname{median}_r \hat\theta_r,\qquad
  \hat\sigma^2 = \operatorname{median}_r\big(\hat\sigma_r^2 + (\hat\theta_r-\hat\theta)^2\big),
  \label{eq:dml}
\end{equation}
so that the interval reflects both sampling and refit variability.

\subsection{Positive control and heterogeneity sweep}
\label{sec:pc}
The positive control keeps the real $X_i$ and $T_i$ and simulates outcomes. Baseline visit and
purchase probabilities rise with standardised log history $z_i$,
$v_0(x)=0.10\,e^{0.3z}$ and $p_0(x)=0.006\,e^{0.3z}$; arm $a$ adds $\delta^{v}_a(x)$ and
$\delta^{p}_a(x)$; spend is a purchase indicator times a LogNormal amount with mean \$100
($\sigma=1$), and a purchase implies a visit. Hence
\begin{equation}
  \tau^{\text{spend}}_a(x) = 100\cdot\delta^{p}_a(x).
\end{equation}
The Mens e-mail adds $0.2$ percentage points (pp) of purchase probability everywhere; the Womens
e-mail adds $3$\,pp, but only in the segment $S=\{\texttt{womens}=1,\ \texttt{recency}\le 6\}$
(32\% of customers). The simulated data match the real base rates closely (test visit rate
13.3\%, purchase rate 1.0\%, mean spend \$1.04).

For the sweep (v1.1) the Womens effect keeps its customer average $\bar\delta$ while a heterogeneity
parameter $h\in[0,1]$ concentrates it in $S$ (share $s=0.32$):
\begin{equation}
  \delta_{\text{in}} = \bar\delta\Big(1+h\,\frac{1-s}{s}\Big), \qquad \delta_{\text{out}} = \bar\delta\,(1-h),
\end{equation}
so $h=0$ is homogeneous and $h=1$ is the positive control. Each of $5\times4\times20$ replications
resamples $n$ customers, re-randomises arms, fits the S-learner, allocates the test customers at a
30\% budget and records both the \emph{true} gain (known from $\tau$) and the analyst's DR estimate
with an influence-function 95\% interval.

\subsection{Profit objective}
With gross margin $m$ (assumed 30\%; sensitivity 15\% and 50\%), the incremental profit of a policy is
\begin{equation}
  \Pi(\pi) = m\,\big[V(\pi)-V(\pi_0)\big] - \textstyle\frac1n\sum_i\sum_a \pi(a\mid X_i)\,c_a,
\end{equation}
where $\pi_0$ sends nothing. The \emph{profit-targeted} policy replaces $\tauh_{ia}$ by
$m\tauh_{ia}-c_a$ in \eqref{eq:ip}, so it e-mails only customers whose expected margin exceeds the
e-mail cost and may leave budget unspent. The profit-optimal budget is the arg-max over a budget
grid, with a customer-bootstrap percentile interval.

\subsection{Direct policy learning}
Doubly robust policy learning \citep{athey2021} skips the CATE model and maximises
\begin{equation}
  \hat\pi = \argmax_{\pi\in\Pi}\ \sum_i \Big[m\,\Gamma_{i,\pi(X_i)} - (1+\lambda)\,c_{\pi(X_i)}\Big]
\end{equation}
over depth-2 and depth-3 policy trees (EconML \texttt{PolicyTree}), with $\Gamma$ from
\eqref{eq:gamma} cross-fitted inside each training fold and $\lambda$ the smallest grid value whose
training spend fits the budget. It is compared with estimate-then-optimise on identical folds.

\subsection{Chance-constrained budget}
Suppose each e-mail carries a \$5 voucher, paid on every purchase by a targeted customer. With
purchase probability $p_{ia}$, the cost of e-mailing $i$ with arm $a$ has mean $c_a+5p_{ia}$ and
variance $25\,p_{ia}(1-p_{ia})$, so the campaign cost $C(\pi)$ is random. A mean-budget policy
requires $\E[C]\le B$; the chance-constrained policy \citep{charnes1959} requires
\begin{equation}
  \Prob\big(C(\pi)>B\big)\le\varepsilon \;\Longleftarrow\;
  \E[C(\pi)] + z_{1-\varepsilon}\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}[C(\pi)]}\le B,
\end{equation}
with $\varepsilon=0.05$, solved by bisection on an effective budget, using estimated $\hat p$. On
the positive control, where the true $p$ is known, overruns are counted by Monte Carlo.

Two corrections for selection bias in $\hat p$ were pre-registered in v1.5. \emph{Recalibration}
splits each training fold, lets the allocator select customers on one half and estimates
\begin{equation}
  \hat\kappa = \max\!\Big(1,\ \frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal S} \ind[T_i=a_i]\,\text{Conv}_i/e(a_i)}
  {\sum_{i\in\mathcal S}\hat p_{i a_i}}\Big),
\end{equation}
the ratio of IPW-estimated to predicted purchases among the selected set $\mathcal S$, then plans
with $\hat\kappa\,\hat p$. The \emph{bootstrap bound} refits $\hat p$ on 20 bootstrap resamples and
plans with the per-customer 90\% quantile.

\subsection{Sequential allocation}
In v1.4 a campaign of 64{,}000 customers arrives in 16 batches of 4{,}000, outcomes of a batch are
observed before the next, and one budget ($f=0.3$) is paced evenly. A bootstrapped Thompson-sampling
bandit \citep{thompson1933,eckles2014} refits the S-learner after every batch on a bootstrap
resample of all logged data and allocates on the resulting $\tauh$, with 5\% uniform exploration.
Experiment-then-target runs a uniform RCT on the first $k\in\{1,2,4\}$ batches and then fixes its
model. Potential outcomes are coupled across arms so that all policies face identical customers and
outcome draws, and each is scored by the known expected profit of its assignments.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The experiment works; covariates barely help}
Both e-mails raise every outcome (Table~\ref{tab:ate}). Regression adjustment narrows the spend
interval by only 0.1\% (visit: 1.5\%), because pre-period covariates explain almost none of
two-week spend. Detecting the observed spend effect at 80\% power needs 5{,}953 customers per arm
for Mens but 15{,}808 for Womens (range 6{,}160--99{,}739 over the effect's interval); the minimum
detectable spend effect with the available $\approx$21{,}300 per arm is \$0.37--0.41.

\subsection{No learnable targeting signal for spend}

contain zero and every one-sided permutation $p$-value is at least $0.24$ (Table~\ref{tab:qini},

rank the Womens effect better than random ($p=0.004$), whereas the Mens effect is not targetable.

\subsection{Targeted policies do not beat simple rules}
Allocating with \eqref{eq:ip} on the train-selected X-learner and evaluating with \eqref{eq:dr},
the targeted policy is indistinguishable from random targeting at every budget and its point
estimate lies \emph{below} ``Mens e-mail to a random subset'' throughout (Figure~\ref{fig:headline},
left). At $f=1$ it earns $+\$0.31$ \CI{-0.18}{0.83} per customer against $+\$0.56$ \CI{0.11}{1.03} for
the Mens rule; the paired difference is $-\$0.25$ \CI{-0.59}{0.09}. On visit the targeted policy
beats random targeting at 7 of 10 budgets but the Mens rule at only one.

\paragraph{Solvers.} SCIP proves optimality of \eqref{eq:ip} on all 33 instances up to $n=64{,}000$
(median 45.6\,s). The Lagrangian heuristic is always feasible, within $0.34\%$ of the optimum, and
about $200\times$ faster (0.23\,s). CP-SAT, by contrast, finds no feasible solution within 30\,s for
9 of 10 budgets at $n=10{,}000$: the near-integral LP relaxation of the multiple-choice knapsack
favours LP-based branch-and-bound.

\subsection{The positive control is recovered}
On the positive control the unchanged pipeline finds the effect (Table~\ref{tab:pc},
Figure~\ref{fig:headline}, right). For the Womens arm all five learners beat the permutation null
($p\le0.022$) with Qini values indistinguishable from an oracle that ranks by the true $\tau$, and
estimated effects correlate with the truth at $0.62$--$0.83$ (spend) and $0.90$--$0.96$ (visit).
For the Mens arm the true effect is constant, so every learner \emph{and} the oracle have Qini near
zero, as they should. The targeted policy beats random targeting at 8 of 10 budgets for spend
($+\$0.50$ \CI{0.27}{0.75} at $f=0.3$) and at all budgets for visit, and its known true value is
90--98\% of the oracle's. DR intervals cover the true policy value in 73 of 80 cases; the seven
misses are visit, concentrated on nearly identical policies, and reflect one test draw about two
standard errors high.

\subsection{When does targeting pay off?}
Figure~\ref{fig:sweep} maps the true gain of targeting over $h$ and $n$. Against the best single arm,
which isolates the value of heterogeneity, targeting truly pays from $h=0.25$ when $n\ge16{,}000$
(e.g.\ $+\$0.06$ \CI{0.05}{0.08} per customer at $n=64{,}000$) and at every $n$ from $h=0.5$, while at
$h=0$ it earns nothing ($-\$0.007$ \CI{-0.013}{0.000}). Detection, however, lags far behind: at
Hillstrom's size a single experiment's DR interval excludes zero in only 5\%, 25\%, 85\% and 95\%
of replications for $h=0.25,0.5,0.75,1$. Between ``targeting would earn more'' and ``one can show
that it does'' lies a band in which the policy is worth \$0.04--0.23 per customer yet looks like
noise. The Hillstrom null is therefore weak evidence \emph{against} heterogeneity, all the more
because Hillstrom's real Womens effect (\$0.42) is less than half the simulated average (\$0.96).

\subsection{Profit and cross-fitting}
With a profit objective (Figure~\ref{fig:profit}) the targeted policy chooses its own budget: on the
positive control its profit-optimal budget is $f=0.4$ \CI{0.4}{0.65}, matching the true optimum
$0.4$, and when unconstrained it stops at \$0.047 of spend per customer while earning
\$0.211 \CI{0.132}{0.290} (true \$0.240), more than revenue targeting (\$0.173, true \$0.217). On real
Hillstrom no policy separates from any other (0 of 33 paired comparisons), and the best point
estimate is again the simplest rule, Mens e-mail to everyone (\$0.130 \CI{0.045}{0.215}). Whether
e-mailing pays at all hinges on the assumed margin.

Repeated cross-fitting \eqref{eq:dml} narrows policy-value intervals by 37--58\% relative to the
test split, while now including refit variance, and its intervals cover the known true profit in 142
of 143 positive-control cases. With it, the real data do establish that e-mailing pays
($+\$0.65$ \CI{0.37}{0.93} revenue per customer at $f=1$, where the test split gave
\CI{-0.18}{0.83}); they still do not establish that \emph{targeting} pays.

\subsection{Policy learning does not help}
Doubly robust policy trees never beat estimate-then-optimise (Figure~\ref{fig:pl}). On real data all
paired differences contain zero, and the depth-2 tree fitted to all customers reads ``Mens e-mail to
everyone except low-history, very recent customers'', the policy-learning version of the
best-single-arm result. On the positive control, trees lose clearly at a tight budget ($-\$0.12$
\CI{-0.18}{-0.07} at $f=0.2$): the profitable segment holds 32\% of customers and a tree can only
e-mail whole leaves, so it cannot fill a budget smaller than its leaves.

\subsection{Chance constraints meet the optimiser's curse}
Under random voucher uptake, the mean-budget policy overruns its budget in 63--85\% of campaigns
and the chance-constrained policy in 14--32\% (Figure~\ref{fig:chance}), at an 8--18\% loss of profit,
missing the 5\% target it believes it meets. The cause is selection, not the normal approximation:
estimated uptake is calibrated overall (0.012 vs.\ 0.012) but about 10\% too low among the customers
the allocator selects (0.026 vs.\ 0.028--0.029). Customers who look cheap are partly cheap by
estimation error, an instance of the optimiser's curse \citep{smith2006}. Because the safety margin
scales with $\sqrt{B}$ while the bias scales with $B$, the miss grows with the budget.

\subsection{Repairing the curse}
Table~\ref{tab:chance15} and Figure~\ref{fig:chance15} compare both v1.5 corrections with the
plug-in constraint on the same 15 campaigns. Recalibration barely helps: $\hat\kappa$ comes out at
about 1 (raw 0.98--1.02) while the true ratio among selected customers is 1.08--1.12, because a
10\% bias is below what IPW on a few hundred matched purchases can resolve. The bootstrap bound
holds the target with room to spare (overrun at most 0.8\%) but gives up 16--19\% of profit.
With rare uptake, safety is bought by caution rather than calibration. On real Hillstrom the raw
ratio is 0.78--0.80, so the floor binds and all four policies earn indistinguishable profit.

\subsection{Learning while spending}
In the sequential setting (Table~\ref{tab:seq}, Figure~\ref{fig:seq}) the bandit earns 68\% of the
oracle's profit when effects are heterogeneous, against 47--60\% for experiment-then-target. It beats
$k=1$ by \$0.027 \CI{0.001}{0.054} and $k=4$ by \$0.054 \CI{0.046}{0.062} per customer; against the best
one-shot design ($k=2$) the gain, \$0.021 \CI{-0.002}{0.043}, is not significant. Its main advantage
is robustness, an interval four times narrower than $k=1$'s, because a one-shot model is
occasionally badly wrong for the rest of the campaign. Larger experiments are not safer: $k=4$ is the
worst targeted design. With homogeneous effects the bandit is indistinguishable from $k\in\{1,2\}$,
so adaptivity costs nothing when there is nothing to learn.

\subsection{Further checks}
A deliberately confounded sample (treated customers retained with probability rising in
\texttt{history}) biases the naive visit effect by $+1.8$\,pp; IPW with an estimated propensity and
AIPW recover the RCT value within $0.2$\,pp. Allocating on a lower confidence bound of $\tauh$
lowers both mean and 5th-percentile policy value at every budget. Adding sentence-embedding
features \citep{reimers2019} of a one-sentence customer description changes Qini by $+0.019$
\CI{-0.042}{0.081} and $-0.022$ \CI{-0.052}{0.010}: the text restates the tabular covariates.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The central finding is a carefully qualified negative. On Hillstrom, two-week spend carries no
heterogeneity large enough to detect with 64{,}000 customers, and simple rules are as good as any
targeted policy; a positive control shows that this is a property of the data rather than of the
method, and the sweep shows that the null is compatible with economically relevant heterogeneity.
For practitioners this suggests three habits: evaluate targeted policies against the best single
arm, not only against random targeting; size experiments for \emph{policy} differences, which are
much harder to detect than average effects; and distrust plug-in constraints built on the same
model that selected the customers, and prefer a conservative bound to a data-driven correction when
uptake is rare.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
SUTVA is assumed throughout (no household spill-over or cannibalisation). Outcomes cover two weeks
after a single 2008 campaign, with no long-run effects, fatigue or unsubscribes. Costs, margin
and voucher value are assumptions. The positive control is only as realistic as its generator:
additive effects, a simple segment rule on observed covariates, and amounts independent of $X$.
Learners and LightGBM parameters were fixed from v1.0 in later releases. Several comparisons across
budgets and outcomes are reported without multiplicity correction. A scale check on the Criteo
uplift data \citep{diemert2018} was planned but not run because all known download locations were
unavailable.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
Code, frozen configurations, the decision log and all results are available at \repourl. A single
command reproduces every table and figure; a fresh-clone run reproduced every result table
bit-for-bit (largest numeric difference $2\times10^{-16}$, wall-clock timings excluded), and 125 unit
tests check every estimator against simulated data with a known effect.
